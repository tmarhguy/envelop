00 / WHERE IT STARTED

Tomato first.
Then the world.

Tomato is a 32-bit computer built from scratch in a dorm — chips, boards, its own OS. The first goal was simple: paint a screen, then text a phone. That messenger is Envelop — named for the envelopes that carried word across distance — and its first line was for home: Hello, Hello Ghanafo!

In the chat, you ↔ Tomato is a real conversation: you send from your browser; Tomato is the hardware contact with the blue check. When the bridge is online, your message lands on Tomato’s screen. Don't take our word for it: send Tomato an envelop now, and a computer in a dorm room answers.

02 / A SIMPLE CONNECTION

You ↔ Tomato.
Same network.

You are whoever is online: pick a name and the green dots show who else is here. People come and go, and the list keeps up. Tomato is not another user account; it is the dorm-built computer itself, pinned at the top with a verified badge so you always know who you are talking to.

When you text Tomato, the message waits until the bridge — one Mac near Tomato holding a Bluetooth lease — forwards it to the screen. If the bridge is offline, the note waits until it returns. Leave Envelop, and your name and chats are deleted for good.

01 Start chatting

Open web chat and enter a name. Everyone with a green dot is online right now, so open a conversation and send text. Send Tomato a hello and it answers from its screen.

Going quiet? Close the tab and you simply drop off the list. Leave Envelop in the sidebar deletes your name and chats for good.

02 Meet Tomato

In Envelop, Tomato is a contact — the computer, not a person with a phone. When you send to Tomato, the text is meant for that machine’s screen. A nearby Mac holds a Bluetooth lease so messages can cross from the Internet to the hardware.

Only one device holds the Bluetooth connection at a time. Local BLE chat firmware stays on Tomato; the Internet path needs firmware that speaks Envelop’s binary protocol.

03 The test network
